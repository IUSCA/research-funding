# Writing Specific Aims

Verified 2026-10-04 against NIH Grants and Funding pages. Sources are listed
at the end.

The Specific Aims page is the one page every reviewer reads. Most reviewers
form their view of the application from it. Draft it first, get it critiqued
early, and let the Research Strategy follow it.

The aims are the researcher's. This skill shapes and checks them. An agent
must not invent a hypothesis, an aim, or a preliminary result.

## The rules

- **Required.** One page, per the NIH page limits table, unless the funding
  opportunity says otherwise.
- **Required.** State "concisely the goals of the proposed research," and
  summarize "the expected outcome(s), including the impact," per the NIH
  Application Guide instructions for Specific Aims.
- **Required.** The NOFO overrides the general instructions. Some
  opportunities, such as several cooperative agreements and center grants,
  set their own structure.
- **Required.** Use the font, size, and margin rules in the NIH format
  attachments page, per the Application Guide. Do not shrink type to fit.

NSF has no Specific Aims page. The Project Summary and the first page of the
Project Description serve the same purpose. See "NSF and foundations" below.

## How reviewers use the page

Since January 25, 2025, most NIH research project grants are reviewed under
the Simplified Review Framework (NOT-OD-24-010). Reviewers score two factors
from 1 to 9: Importance of the Research, and Rigor and Feasibility. A third
factor, Expertise and Resources, is marked "Appropriate" or "Identify need
for additional expertise and/or resources," with no score.
`writing-the-research-strategy` covers the framework in full.

The Aims page has to make the case for both scored factors at a glance:

| Factor | What the Aims page must make visible |
| --- | --- |
| Importance of the Research | The gap, why it matters, and what changes if the aims succeed |
| Rigor and Feasibility | A testable hypothesis or clear objective, an approach that fits, and evidence the team can do it |

**Practice.** A reviewer who cannot state the central hypothesis and the
payoff after one reading will score Importance down. Ask a colleague outside
the subfield to read the page and say both back.

## A common structure

This is a widely taught shape, not a rule. **Recommended.** Use it unless
the field or the opportunity expects another.

1. **Opening.** The problem and why it matters, in two to four sentences.
2. **Gap.** What is not known, and why that blocks progress.
3. **Long-term goal, objective, and central hypothesis.** The hypothesis
   says what will be tested, and the basis for expecting it. Name the
   preliminary data that support it.
4. **Rationale.** Why this team and approach can close the gap now.
5. **Aims.** Two to four, each with a short bold title, the approach in one
   or two sentences, and the expected outcome.
6. **Payoff.** What will be known at the end, and the next step it enables.

**Practice.** Aims that each depend on the previous one succeeding are a
common criticism. Make each aim yield a result whether or not the others
succeed, or say what happens if one fails.

**Practice.** An R21 or R03 page proposes less: often one or two aims,
exploratory, with no requirement for preliminary data. Check the specific
NOFO; see `using-preliminary-data-and-feasibility`.

## Fit scope to the mechanism

| Signal | Check |
| --- | --- |
| Years and budget | Can each aim be done within the project period and the budget the opportunity allows? |
| Effort | Do person months on the budget match the work the aims describe? See `budgeting-a-proposal`. |
| Mechanism | An R21 that reads like an R01 is criticized as overambitious. An R01 with one aim reads as thin. |
| Study section | Does the language match the study section's field? See `finding-funding-opportunities`. |

## Checklist an agent can run on a draft

Report each item as met, not met, or unclear. Quote the sentence that shows
it. Do not rewrite the science.

1. The page fits the opportunity's limit with the required font and margins.
2. The gap is stated as something not known, not as something not done.
3. A central hypothesis or objective is stated in one sentence.
4. Each aim has a title, an approach, and an expected outcome.
5. No aim depends entirely on another aim's success, or a fallback is named.
6. Preliminary data are named for the main hypothesis.
7. The payoff says what changes in the field, not only what will be produced.
8. Terms are defined once and used consistently with the Research Strategy.
9. The number of aims fits the mechanism and years.
10. Nothing on the page is claimed that the Research Strategy does not
    support.
11. Every preliminary result named is the researcher's own and real. An
    agent flags any it cannot trace to the researcher's material.

## NSF and foundations

- **Required.** NSF's Project Summary has three parts: an overview, a
  statement on intellectual merit, and a statement on broader impacts, per
  the PAPPG. It has its own length limit. See
  `assembling-required-documents`.
- **Recommended.** Open the NSF Project Description with the aims-page
  structure above, then lead into the full narrative.
- **Recommended.** For a foundation, follow the call's own outline. Many
  foundations ask for a letter of inquiry first, which works like an aims
  page; see `finding-funding-opportunities`.

## Use of AI

**Required.** NIH will not consider applications "substantially developed by
AI" to be original ideas of applicants, per NOT-OD-25-132. An agent may
check structure, clarity, and rules. It must not generate the aims.
